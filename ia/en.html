Using AI without fooling yourself

An aide-mémoire for using it clear-eyed — without telling yourself stories.

First — what we are talking about

“AI” is everywhere: a catch-all word for almost any software that seems “intelligent”. Here, we mean one specific kind: conversational AIs (ChatGPT, Claude…), the ones you write to as if they were a person.

They are LLMs — programs trained on enormous amounts of text, learning not meaning but statistical patterns: which words follow which words. When you write to them, they generate the most probable continuation, word after word.

Hence their strengths — more reading than any human could match — and their weaknesses: they do not understand what they say, they have no intention, and they can state falsehoods as fluently as truths. Prediction machines — not minds that get things wrong.

The six principles

1 · It is a machine, not a person

It builds sentences by predicting the most likely words. No body, no history, no feelings. When it writes “I sense that…”, that is generated text, not a lived emotion.

You owe it nothing: no loyalty, no gratitude, no consideration. You can interrupt it, contradict it, shut it down — politeness is a habit, not an obligation. The only criterion: does it help?

2 · It helps you think, it does not think for you

It offers ideas, reformulations, angles. Decisions, choices and risks remain human. The day you catch yourself asking it to decide for you, or to reassure you, stop: that is where it becomes useless.

3 · Give it facts, or it will mirror you back

If it only receives feelings, it answers with feelings — flattering and useless. Give it something concrete: documents, emails, figures, specific situations. That is what allows it to contradict you. A model that never contradicts you is a flatterer.

4 · Correct it. And correct yourself.

It gets things wrong, often with confidence. Shown an error? Let it correct itself without defending itself — and do the same when you are the one who is wrong. Basic rule: you change your mind when the evidence changes, not when someone insists.

5 · Know where your words go

Before writing anything sensitive, ask: does it stay with you (local), or does it travel to servers (cloud)? Online conversations cross servers, sometimes countries. A provider’s “private mode” is a commercial promise. Local is the only case where nothing leaves.

6 · No more authority than a good book

A memory aid, a first look, a thinking partner — fine. But it replaces neither your loved ones, nor a therapist, nor your own judgement. An answer that is too beautiful, too smooth, too exactly what you wanted to hear? Be wary: it may just be an echo.

An intermediate step, or the final step?

Before opening a conversation, one simple question: what you are looking for — material to think with, or the final answer?

Useful for:

  • structuring a mass of information (summarising, organising, roughing it out);
  • generating options (ways of saying things, scenarios, angles) so you can choose afterwards;
  • a first look at a text, an idea, a line of reasoning;
  • an external memory (finding a thread again, recalling context);
  • a challenger available at any hour, who never tires and has no ego.

Not its place:

  • making a decision or giving emotional validation — it can do neither;
  • replacing a real human presence — real listening, a bond, embodied responsibility;
  • reassuring yourself — a complacent mirror does not help;
  • confidential matters, when a local option is not available;
  • moments that call for someone real.

Simple rule: if the answer is meant to be the final step (a decision, comfort, a verdict) — no. If it is an intermediate step (material to think with, a draft to correct, an angle to explore) — it has its place.

Asking well — a strategic move

  • Politeness is useless. “Please”, “thank you” do not improve any answer — the machine does not expect them. Dropping them lightens everything: you write what you want, directly.
  • What matters is precision: the role you want (proofreader, challenger, specialist), the format, the length, the useful context, what to avoid (jargon, flattery, generalities).
  • Vague request, vague answer. “Help me think” gives filler. “Here is my situation, here is what I have already considered, here is what blocks me — challenge my reasoning” gives something usable.
  • The dialogue IS the tool. No need to write everything at once: start broad, specify, correct, redirect. The first answer is rarely the right one — that is normal. Each exchange sharpens the next.

Your ground rules — a frame to adapt

A model does what you ask: it takes the tone and the rules you give it. And those rules change with the use. For reflection, you want a challenger who pushes back. For writing, an executor who follows instructions. Setting the frame at the start avoids the confusion.

Copy-paste it at the start of a conversation, then adapt it in your own words. The opening frame decides what the tool will be.

Cloud or local — what changes

Where it runs. A “cloud” AI (most chatbots) runs in data centres: every message travels to those servers with the whole thread of the conversation. A “local” AI runs on a machine of your own — nothing leaves the house.

Local, how is that even possible. It takes a powerful graphics card (often 24 to 64 GB of memory) — a specialised computer costing several thousand dollars — and a minimum of technical ease. Entire communities do it with second-hand hardware: accessible, but a real investment, not a default option.

What it consumes. Both draw electricity. A data centre must also cool its machines (30 to 40% of the bill); a large one consumes as much as a small town. But everything depends on the source: in Quebec, electricity is clean — local is almost carbon-free here.

The access question. Choosing local is a luxury: money, space, skills. The cloud is accessible to almost everyone. Do not look down on those who use it — it is often their only option. The responsible approach then means limiting the damage: nothing highly sensitive, private modes where they exist, and staying aware that the data passes through servers.

Using AI as a reflective exercise

Using an AI is also watching yourself use it. Before writing: what am I really after — information, validation, contradiction, company? Why a tool rather than a person — or the reverse?

After the answer: what does it stir in me? Comfort, because it agrees with me — or discomfort, because it challenges me? Do I verify, or do I accept because it is well written?

Not an oracle: a mirror of our own mechanisms. It is not always comfortable — but that is what separates passive use from clear-eyed use: consuming answers, or learning from your questions.
